Helping the brain find new routes after stroke.

Octopus Lab studies how the brain recovers and adapts after a lesion. We combine non-invasive brain stimulation with speech-language therapy to improve post-stroke aphasia, and use neuroimaging to see what the stimulation changes in the brain.

Why an octopus

A nervous system built to adapt

An octopus has about 500 million neurons, and roughly two-thirds of them sit in its arms rather than its central brain. It is a model of distributed, resilient control, and that is how we think about the brain after a stroke.

Octopus: each arm can sense, decide and act with a degree of independence.

→ The brain

Distributed networks

Language does not live in a single spot. It runs on large networks spread across both hemispheres. When one region is damaged, other parts of the network can take on part of the work.

Octopus: a lost arm grows back, with its nerves and suckers.

→ The brain

Recovery and plasticity

The adult brain cannot regrow tissue the same way, but it can rewire. Months and years after a stroke, connections still change with therapy, and recovery is still possible.

Octopus: it changes colour, shape and strategy to fit its surroundings.

→ The brain

Resilience

No two strokes are alike, so no single treatment fits everyone. We tailor where and how we stimulate to each person's language profile and lesion, so the brain can adapt in its own way.

The arms of our logo are the brain's connections. Stimulation and therapy help them reach new paths.

Research

Brain stimulation, therapy and imaging, together

Speech-language therapy is the gold standard for aphasia, but it rarely leads to full recovery. We test whether pairing it with targeted brain stimulation can do more, and we measure how the brain changes in response.

What we study

  • Post-stroke aphasia, especially word-finding and semantic difficulties, and the language networks that support recovery.
  • Other post-stroke impairments that may respond to the same stimulation-plus-therapy approach.
  • Who benefits, and why. How lesion location and brain connectivity predict response to stimulation.

Where we are heading

  • Personalized targets. Choosing the stimulation site from each person's language profile rather than one site for everyone.
  • Clinically usable protocols. Short, well-tolerated stimulation that fits into real therapy sessions.
  • Research that reaches the clinic, built with speech-language pathologists and people living with aphasia.
Stimulation

rTMS

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, including theta-burst protocols that take under three minutes, to boost or quiet a targeted brain region before therapy.

Stimulation

tDCS

Transcranial direct current stimulation, including high-definition montages, to shift the excitability of language areas during training.

Imaging

EEG

Electroencephalography to track fast changes in brain activity and connectivity across sessions of stimulation and therapy.

Imaging

fMRI

Task-based and resting-state functional MRI, with lesion-symptom and connectome mapping, to see where stimulation changes the network.

Clinical trial

The ATLAS study

ATLAS tests rTMS targeting the anterior temporal lobe (ATL), a hub for word meaning, combined with speech-language therapy for people with word-finding difficulties after stroke.

Now recruiting · Columbia, SC

Who
Adults with mild-to-moderate aphasia at least 6 months after a left-hemisphere stroke, right-handed, native English speakers, with word-finding difficulties.
Visits
10 sessions over 2 weeks (5 days a week). Each session: rTMS lasting under 3 minutes, then 30 minutes of therapy.
Tests
Language assessments before, right after, and one month after treatment (about 2 hours each), plus two MRI scans.
Why ATL
Most stimulation studies target Broca's area. ATLAS is among the first to target the ATL and pair it with a therapy built on word meaning.

Interested in taking part? Contact Dr. Arheix-Parras at sophie.arheix-parras@sc.edu. Pilot work was supported by the Barbara Martin Aphasia Research Grant from the National Aphasia Association.

PhD positions for Fall 2027

I am recruiting PhD students to start in Fall 2027, when Octopus Lab opens at UT Dallas. If you are curious about how the brain recovers after stroke and want hands-on experience with brain stimulation and neuroimaging, I would like to hear from you.

You might be a good fit if you have

  • A background in speech-language pathology, neuroscience, psychology, cognitive science, linguistics, or biomedical engineering.
  • Interest in aphasia, stroke rehabilitation, or brain plasticity.
  • Curiosity about methods. Prior experience with TMS, tDCS, EEG, MRI or programming is welcome but not required.

What you will do

  • Run clinical studies with people living with aphasia, from design to publication.
  • Learn rTMS, tDCS, EEG and fMRI analysis, including lesion and connectivity mapping.
  • Work alongside clinicians so that research questions come from real therapy needs.

Students apply through the Speech, Language, and Hearing Sciences PhD program in the School of Behavioral and Brain Sciences. Full-time PhD students are considered for assistantship stipends, tuition scholarships and health insurance. The GRE is not required.
